# sourced
1 | By the Kebar River in Babylon, the priest Ezekiel sees a storm, four living creatures with wheels full of eyes, and above them a throne with “the appearance of the likeness of Yahweh’s glory”. || The word of the Lord first came to Ezekiel in 593 BC, the fifth year of Jehoiachin’s exile [[tosn:Ezekiel 1:2]]. The wheels belong to a divine war chariot [[tosn:Ezekiel 1:15]]; God revealed himself in Babylon, against all expectations [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
2 | God sends Ezekiel to rebellious Israel: “whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse.” || God often calls Ezekiel “son of man” (ben-adam), meaning a human being, a mortal made from dust [[tosn:Ezekiel 2:3]]. Being a prophet was not a career one chose [[tosn:Ezekiel 2:1–10]].
3 | Ezekiel eats a scroll that tastes sweet as honey, and is appointed a watchman for Israel. || The scroll tasted sweet as honey, like God’s words in Psalm 119:103 [[tosn:Ezekiel 3:2–3]]; John eats a scroll in Revelation 10:9–10 [[bible:Revelation 10:9-10]]. As watchman, Ezekiel had to warn the people of danger [[tosn:Ezekiel 3:16–19]].
4 | Ezekiel acts out the siege of Jerusalem with a clay tablet and lies on his side for many days. || The first sign act: a model of Jerusalem under siege [[tosn:Ezekiel 4:1–2]]. Chapters 4–5 hold four symbolic acts [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
5 | Ezekiel shaves his head and divides the hair into three parts, picturing the fate of Jerusalem’s people. || Shaving his head and beard with a sword pictured the coming destruction, and the scattered third pictured exile [[tosn:Ezekiel 5:1–4]] [[tosn:Ezekiel 5:2]].
6 | Prophecy against the mountains of Israel and their idols. || In exile the people would see their unfaithfulness and know that the Lord alone is God [[tosn:Ezekiel 6:8–10]].
7 | “The end has come.” Disaster is near for the land. || The judgement widens from Israel’s mountains to the whole land [[tosn:Ezekiel 7:1–2]] [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
8 | In a vision Ezekiel is taken to Jerusalem and sees idolatry in the temple: elders worshipping images, women weeping for Tammuz, men worshipping the sun. || Women weep for Tammuz (Dumuzi), a Babylonian god whose yearly death was mourned to bring back fertility [[tosn:Ezekiel 8:14–15]]. Idolatry had reached the temple courts [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
9 | A man with a writing case marks those who grieve over the city’s sins before judgement falls. || The man in linen marks the faithful for safety, like the blood at the first Passover [[tosn:Ezekiel 9:4–6]]. Revelation 7:3 also seals God’s servants on their foreheads [[bible:Revelation 7:3]].
10 | The glory of Yahweh rises from the temple to the threshold, carried by cherubim. || The vision of God’s glory, first seen in Babylon (ch. 1), now appears in the Jerusalem temple [[tosn:Ezekiel 10:1–2]]. When God’s presence left Jerusalem, exile was near [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
11 | The glory leaves the city. God promises to be a sanctuary to the exiles and to give them “one heart” and “a new spirit”. || Israel’s future lay with the exiles, for God himself would be their sanctuary [[tosn:Ezekiel 11:15–16]]. The promise of a new heart and renewed fellowship is developed in chapters 33–48 [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
12 | Ezekiel packs an exile’s bag and digs through a wall as a sign of the coming exile. || Digging through the wall foreshadows Zedekiah’s attempted escape and capture [[tosn:Ezekiel 12:5–7]] [[tosn:Ezekiel 12:12–13]].
13 | Against false prophets who whitewash a flimsy wall and cry “Peace” when there is none. || False prophets made up their own messages; true prophets spoke the word of the Lord [[tosn:Ezekiel 13:1–3]].
14 | Even Noah, Daniel and Job could save only themselves. || Noah, Daniel and Job each stayed faithful in a wicked generation [[tosn:Ezekiel 14:14]]. Because Ezekiel’s spelling differs slightly, some question whether this is the prophet Daniel, but Tyndale thinks it likely [[tosn:Ezekiel 14:14]].
15 | Jerusalem is like a useless vine branch, fit only for burning. || Vine wood is useless, even as firewood [[tosn:Ezekiel 15:1–5]]; so Jerusalem would be completely burned [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]]. Jesus calls himself the true vine (John 15:1) [[bible:John 15:1]].
16 | A long allegory: God found Jerusalem as an abandoned baby, raised her and married her, but she became unfaithful. Yet God will remember his covenant and make atonement. || God adopted Jerusalem like an abandoned baby girl, washed her and made a covenant with her, but she was unfaithful [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]]. The city began with Canaanite roots [[tosn:Ezekiel 16:1–3]].
17 | A riddle of two eagles and a vine about Zedekiah’s rebellion; God will plant a tender twig on a high mountain. || The first eagle is Nebuchadnezzar and the cedar sprig is Jehoiachin [[tosn:Ezekiel 17:11–18]]. God will plant a new shoot of David’s line that grows into a great cedar [[tosn:Ezekiel 17:22–24]].
18 | “The soul who sins, he shall die”: each person is responsible for their own sin. God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked: “Turn yourselves, and live!” || The proverb about sour grapes claimed children suffer for their parents’ sins [[tosn:Ezekiel 18:1–2]]. God’s answer: the person who sins is the one who dies [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
19 | A lament over the princes of Israel, pictured as lion cubs captured and a vine uprooted. || A funeral song for Judah’s princes [[tosn:Ezekiel 19:1–14]]. The lioness is Hamutal, Josiah’s wife, mother of Jehoahaz and Zedekiah [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
20 | God recounts Israel’s rebellion from Egypt onward and promises to bring them out as a new exodus and purify them. || Spoken five years before Jerusalem fell [[tosn:Ezekiel 20:1–3]]. God reviews Israel’s history of rebellion since Egypt [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
21 | The sword of Babylon is sharpened against Jerusalem. The crown will be removed “until he comes whose right it is”. || Many read the one “whose right it is” as the Messiah, as in Genesis 49:10, though here Ezekiel applies it to the coming judgement through Nebuchadnezzar [[tosn:Ezekiel 21:25–27]].
22 | Jerusalem, “the bloody city”, is condemned; God looked for someone to stand in the gap, but found none. || Like a prosecutor, Ezekiel lists Jerusalem’s crimes, starting with shedding innocent blood [[tosn:Ezekiel 22:1–5]]. Prophets, priests and princes were all corrupt [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
23 | An allegory of two sisters, Oholah (Samaria) and Oholibah (Jerusalem), and their unfaithfulness. || Like chapter 16, it retells the history of Samaria and Jerusalem as two unfaithful sisters [[tosn:Ezekiel 23:1–49]]; marriage pictures the covenant [[tosn:Ezekiel 23:4]].
24 | The siege begins. Ezekiel’s wife, “the desire of your eyes”, dies, and he is told not to mourn, as a sign. || The siege of Jerusalem begins [[tosn:Ezekiel 24:2]]. No prophet is so deeply involved in his message: God takes Ezekiel’s wife, and he may not mourn openly [[tosn:Ezekiel 24:15–17]].
25 | Oracles against Ammon, Moab, Edom and the Philistines for gloating over Judah. || Chapters 25–32 address the nations: six short messages to Judah’s neighbours, then Egypt [[tosn:Ezekiel 25:1–32.32]]. They form a bridge between the book’s two halves [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
26 | Tyre will be destroyed and become a bare rock for drying nets. || Tyre prepared to profit from Jerusalem’s fall [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]]. Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for 13 years; Alexander the Great destroyed it in 332 BC [[tosn:Ezekiel 26:12–14]].
27 | A lament for Tyre pictured as a splendid merchant ship that sinks. || Tyre’s trade network was vast [[tosn:Ezekiel 27:12–25]]. Revelation 18 echoes this lament in the fall of Babylon [[bible:Revelation 18:11-19]].
28 | Against the king of Tyre, who says “I am a god”; he was in Eden, a guardian cherub, until pride corrupted him. || With Isaiah 14, this is sometimes read as Satan’s fall, but it first describes a proud ruler; such powers may serve Satan yet fall [[tosn:Ezekiel 28:1–19]]. Interpreters differ on whether the “king” of Tyre is the prince or Satan [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
29 | Against Pharaoh, the great monster in the Nile. Egypt will be humbled. || The longest message against the nations targets Egypt [[tosn:Ezekiel 29:1–32.32]]. Nebuchadnezzar’s army worked hard at Tyre but gained little, so Egypt is his wages [[tosn:Ezekiel 29:18–20]].
30 | The day of Yahweh against Egypt and its allies. || A lament repeating much of 29:1–16: Egypt and its allies will fall [[tosn:Ezekiel 30:1–19]].
31 | Pharaoh is compared to a mighty cedar of Lebanon, like Assyria, cut down for its pride. || Assyria, once like a great cedar in Eden, was cut down; how could Egypt boast? [[tosn:Ezekiel 31:1–18]] [[tosn:Ezekiel 31:2–4]].
32 | A lament over Pharaoh and a vision of the nations descending to the pit. || Pharaoh is a lion and a sea monster that God will hunt [[tosn:Ezekiel 32:2–3]]. The lament is dated two months after news of Jerusalem’s fall [[tosn:Ezekiel 32:1]].
33 | Ezekiel is again appointed watchman. News arrives: “The city has been defeated!” || The book turns to renewal [[tosn:Ezekiel 33:1–48.35]]. A survivor brings news of Jerusalem’s fall, over five months later [[tosn:Ezekiel 33:21]].
34 | Against Israel’s selfish shepherds who feed themselves. God himself will search for his sheep, and set over them one shepherd, “my servant David”. || The shepherds are Judah’s former kings; in the ancient Near East a shepherd stood for a king [[tosn:Ezekiel 34:1–24]]. Chapter 34 is the key to the messages of restoration [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]]. Jesus calls himself the good shepherd [[bible:John 10:11]].
35 | Against Mount Seir (Edom) for its hatred of Israel. || Edom, also called Mount Seir, stands for all Israel’s enemies and rejoiced at Israel’s fall [[tosn:Ezekiel 35:1–15]].
36 | God will bring Israel home for his name’s sake: “I will also give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit within you… I will put my Spirit within you” || God will give a new heart and a new spirit [[tosn:Ezekiel 36:26]]. In the New Testament the Gentiles too receive the Holy Spirit [[tosn:Ezekiel 36:16–38]].
37 | The valley of dry bones: at God’s word the bones come together, and breath enters them. Two sticks joined into one picture the reunited kingdom under one king. || The dry bones show God’s people without hope; God will renew them [[tosn:Ezekiel 37:2]] [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]]. The Messiah’s rule will be everlasting (37:25) [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
38 | Gog of the land of Magog leads a great attack on restored Israel and is defeated by God. || Gog leads seven nations, a picture of an unbeatable alliance [[tosn:Ezekiel 38:1–6]]. Some link Gog with Gyges of Lydia, but the text goes beyond any one ruler [[tosn:Ezekiel 38:1–39.29]]. Revelation 20:8 names Gog and Magog [[bible:Revelation 20:8]].
39 | Gog’s defeat, and God pours out his Spirit on Israel: “I won’t hide my face from them any more” || Israel burns the enemy’s weapons for seven years and buries the dead for seven months; seven means completeness [[tosn:Ezekiel 39:9–10]] [[tosn:Ezekiel 39:12–16]].
40 | In a vision Ezekiel is taken to a high mountain and shown a new temple, measured by a man with a measuring rod. || The temple vision is dated the 25th year of exile [[bible:Ezekiel 40:1]], twenty years after Ezekiel’s call in 593 BC [[tosn:Ezekiel 1:2]]. The guide’s measuring tools are for building, not destroying [[tosn:Ezekiel 40:3]]; Revelation 21:15 also measures the new Jerusalem [[bible:Revelation 21:15]].
41 | The measurements of the inner temple, with carvings of cherubim and palm trees. || Only priests could enter the inner court; the thick walls showed strength [[tosn:Ezekiel 41:1–2]].
42 | The rooms for the priests and the outer walls separating holy and common. || The priests’ rooms held the most sacred offerings and their holy clothes [[tosn:Ezekiel 42:13–14]].
43 | The glory of God returns to the temple from the east: “This is the place of my throne.” || God’s glory, which left in chapter 10, returns from the east [[tosn:Ezekiel 43:1–4]] [[tbd:005:Ezekiel, Book of]].
44 | The east gate stays shut because God entered by it; rules for priests and the Levites. || The Zadokite priests stayed loyal when others turned to idols; Zadok was high priest under Solomon [[tosn:Ezekiel 44:15–16]].
45 | A holy district is set apart for the sanctuary, the priests and the prince, with laws for just weights. || The sacred land at the centre included land for the priests [[tosn:Ezekiel 45:1–8]].
46 | Rules for worship on Sabbaths and festivals, and the prince’s offerings. || The prince represents the people in worship and joins their procession [[tosn:Ezekiel 46:1–15]].
47 | A river flows from the temple, growing deeper, turning the Dead Sea fresh and teeming with fish; trees on its banks bear fruit every month, with leaves for healing. || The river from the temple recalls the river in Eden and turns death into life, even freshening the Dead Sea [[tosn:Ezekiel 47:1]] [[tosn:Ezekiel 47:6–9]]. Revelation 22:1–2 pictures the river of life [[bible:Revelation 22:1-2]].
48 | The land is divided among the tribes, and the city is named “Yahweh is there”. || The city’s new name, “Yahweh is there”, completes the vision: the Lord who once left Jerusalem has returned [[tosn:Ezekiel 48:35]].
